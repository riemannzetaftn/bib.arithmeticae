# Section 1. Generalities

Recall that a field \(F\) is a commutative ring with \(1\neq 0\), whose every nonzero element is invertible (w.r.t multiplication). We have the following basic observations.

- If \(I\) is a nonzero ideal of \(F\), let \(a\in I\setminus\{0\}\). Then for any \(b\in F\), \(b = (ba^{-1})a\in ba^{-1}I\subset I\), so that \(I = F\). Thus we see that only ideals of \(F\) are \((0)\) and \((1)\).
- Therefore, if \(\varphi:F\to K\) is a morphism of fields (a unital ring homomorphism between the fields), then the ideal \(\varphi^{-1}((0))\) of \(F\) do not contain \(1\), hence must be \((0)\). That is, \(\varphi\) is injective, and we can identify it with the inclusion of a subfield, up to an isomorphism.

In the theory of fields, we often focus on the codomain, and this leads to the following definition.

!!! definition "Field Extension"

    When \(F\) is a subfield of a field \(K\) (i.e. a subset which is a field under inherited binary operations), we say that \(K\) is a *(field) extension* of \(F\). Similarly, we say that \(K\) is a field over \(F\), or \(K/F\) is an extension of fields.

This is more or less a morphism of fields, with emphasis on the codomain, rather than the domain or the morphism itself. As a first example, we introduce the prime subfields.

!!! definition "Characteristic and Prime Subfields"

    The *characteristic* of a field \(F\), denoted by \(\char(F)\), is defined by the unique non-negative generator of the kernel of the universal ring homomorphism \(\bbZ\to F\), which is either \(0\) or a prime number \(p\). If \(\char(F) = 0\), then there is unique morphism of fields \(\bbQ\to F\), and if \(\char(F) = p\), there is unique morphism of fields \(\bbF_p\to F\). In each case, the embedded field (\(\bbQ\) or \(\bbF_p\)) is called the *prime subfield* of \(F\). The fields \(\bbQ, \bbF_p\) themselves are called the *prime fields*.

Let us check the well-definedness.

- The kernel is the preimage of the prime ideal \((0)\) of \(F\), hence a prime ideal of \(\bbZ\), which is either \((0)\) or \((p)\) for a prime number \(p\).
- If the characteristic is \(0\), this means that the ring homomorphism \(\bbZ\to F\) is injective, hence there is unique morphism of fields \(\bbQ\to F\), by the universal property of field of fractions. If the characteristic is \(p\), then there is unique ring homomorphism \(\bbZ/p\bbZ\to F\) which is injective, where \(\bbZ/p\bbZ = \bbF_p\).
- Note that the morphism of fields, being injective, preserves the characteristic. Hence there are no morphism between fields of different characteristic, and we have disconnected full subcategories of \(\Field\), namely, \(\Field_0\), \(\Field_2, \Field_3, \cdots\), each consisting of fields of given characteristic. Then the prime fields are initial objects of the subcategories.

Note that the intersection of subfields of a field \(F\) is again a subfield of \(F\): hence the following definition arises.

!!! definition "Generated Subfield"

    Given a subset \(S\) of a field \(F\), the intersection of all subfields of \(F\) containing \(S\) is the smallest subfield of \(F\) containing \(S\), called the *subfield of \(F\) generated by \(S\)*. In particular, if \(F\) is a subfield of \(K\) and \(S\) is a subset of \(K\), the subfield of \(K\) generated by \(F\cup S\) is denoted by \(F(S)\), and called the *subfield of \(K\) generated by \(S\) over \(F\)*, or the *subextension of \(K/F\) generated by \(S\)*. If \(S\) is finite, then we say that the subfield \(F(S)\) is *finitely generated over \(F\)*, or the subextension \(F(S)/F\) is *finitely generated*. If \(S\) is a singleton, \(F(S)/F\) is called a *simple extension*. Finally, if \(E,F\) are subfields of \(K\), the subfield \(EF := F(E) = E(F)\) of \(K\) generated by \(E\cup F\) is called the *compositum* of \(E\) and \(F\).

If \(S = \{\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n\}\) is finite, we also write \(F(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n)\) for \(F(S)\). The observations follow.

- The latter definition in fact embraces the general case, as we may take \(F\) to be the prime subfield; since every subfield contains \(F\), the containment is vacuous.
- The field \(F(S)\) has more concrete description. There is a universal morphism of rings \(F[S]\to K\) (the domain is the polynomial ring with variables in \(S\) and coefficients in \(F\)), and its image (a subring) in \(F\) is also denoted by \(F[S]\) by abuse of notation. Note that the kernel might be nontrivial, hence a nonzero polynomial in \(S\) might vanish in \(K\) (this is an algebraic relation of elements of \(S\)). Then the field of fractions of the subring \(F[S]\) is \(F(S)\), which consists of all rational expressions in \(S\) with coefficients in \(F\), whose denominator do not vanish in \(K\). It is a subfield of \(K\) by definition, it contains \(F\) and \(S\), and clearly every subfield of \(K\) containing \(F\cup S\) must contain the elements of \(F(S)\).
- When \(S\) is any set and \(F\) is any field, there is a field \(F(S)\) of  rational functions with variables in \(S\) and coefficients in \(F\). The coincidence of notation is unfortunate, but indeed \(F(S)/F\) is a subextension of itself generated by \(S\), so this is a special case of above. This defines a free functor \(\Set\to\Field\), and one may say that this field is freely generated by \(S\).
- If \(S, T\) are two subsets of \(K\), then one can easily see that \(F(S\cup T) = F(S)(T) = F(T)(S)\). In the finite case, this leads to inductive formula \(F(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n) = F(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_{n-1})(\alpha_n)\).
